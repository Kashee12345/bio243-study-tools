    Why can a deeper burn hurt less?

    Pain is a message sent by nerve endings (free nerve endings and other sensory receptors) in the dermis. In first- and second-degree burns these endings are irritated but still alive, so they fire hard. That is why a blistered burn hurts so much.

    A full-thickness (third-degree) burn destroys the whole dermis, nerve endings included. With no receptors left, the centre goes numb. The edges are shallower, so they still hurt.

    Think of it like this: a smoke alarm screams while there is smoke. If the fire burns the alarm itself, the house goes quiet. Quiet does not mean safe.

    Why is losing skin so dangerous?

    Fluid balance. Intact skin keeps water in. Burned capillaries leak plasma, and the open wound lets water evaporate. A large burn can cause dehydration, electrolyte imbalance and even shock or kidney failure.

    Temperature control. Burned skin cannot sweat or adjust blood flow normally, and wet wounds lose heat by evaporation. Patients can get dangerously cold.

    Infection defence. The epidermis is a physical barrier. Once it is gone, bacteria can get in, and dead burned tissue (eschar) has no blood supply to bring immune cells.

    The Rule of Nines (adult)

    • Head and neck: 9% (4.5 front, 4.5 back)
    • Each upper limb: 9% (4.5 front, 4.5 back)
    • Anterior trunk: 18% (chest 9, abdomen 9)
    • Posterior trunk: 18% (upper back 9, lower back and buttocks 9)
    • Each lower limb: 18% (9 front, 9 back)
    • Perineum and genitalia: 1%

    Eleven areas of 9% plus 1% gives 100%.

    What counts

    Count only partial-thickness (second-degree) and full-thickness (third- and fourth-degree) burns. First-degree (superficial) burns are not counted in %TBSA, because the skin barrier is still intact.

    Palm method: the patient's own palm, including the fingers, is about 1% of their TBSA. It is handy for small or scattered burns.

    Children are different. An infant's head is much bigger for its body (about 18 to 19% instead of 9%), and the legs are smaller. Clinicians use age-based charts such as the Lund-Browder chart for children.

    The Parkland formula

    After a big burn, fluid pours out of leaky capillaries. Replacing it fast prevents shock. The Parkland formula estimates how much IV fluid (lactated Ringer's) to give in the first 24 hours.

    4 mL × body weight (kg) × %TBSA = mL of lactated Ringer's in the first 24 h
    • Half goes in the first 8 hours, counted from the time of the burn (not from arrival at the hospital).
    • The other half goes in over the next 16 hours.

    It is only an estimate: the actual rate is adjusted hour by hour based on the patient's urine output, and local protocols vary.

    Why it connects to A&P

    • Skin keeps water in. Lose skin, lose water.
    • Heat makes capillaries leaky, so plasma moves out of the blood.
    • Less blood volume means lower blood pressure and less blood to organs such as the kidneys.
    • Urine output tells the team whether the kidneys are getting enough blood.

    Common mistakes

    • Counting first-degree burns in %TBSA.
    • Starting the 8-hour clock at arrival instead of the time of the burn.
    • Using pounds instead of kilograms (1 kg = 2.2 lb).
