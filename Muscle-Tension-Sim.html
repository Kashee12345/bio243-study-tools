Mini demo · Treppe (the staircase effect)

A muscle that has been resting gets the same stimulus over and over, slowly enough that it fully relaxes each time. The first few twitches get a little stronger each time, like a staircase. This is treppe (German for "staircase"). The usual explanation: Ca²⁺ builds up in the sarcoplasm a bit more with each twitch, so more is available to bind troponin. Treppe is different from summation: here the muscle relaxes completely between twitches.

Same stimulus strength every time. Only the twitch height changes.

Why there is a "just right" length

Tension100%

Tension comes from cross-bridges: myosin heads grabbing actin. A myosin head can only pull if a thin filament is lying right next to it. So the tension a sarcomere can make depends on how much the thick and thin filaments overlap before it starts to contract. This is the length–tension relationship.

  • Too short: thin filaments from both sides run into each other in the middle, and the thick filaments press against the Z discs. There is no room left to slide. Less tension.
  • Optimal: about 80–120% of resting length. The peak sits near 2.0–2.25 µm, where every myosin head has a thin filament to grab.
  • Overstretched: the filaments barely overlap. Few heads can reach actin, so few cross-bridges form. Low tension. With no overlap at all, no tension.

Your skeleton helps here: bones and joints keep most muscles near their best length.

Think of it like this

A tug-of-war team on a rope. If the team bunches up so close that they trip over each other, they pull badly. If the rope is so far away that only two people can grab it, they pull badly. The best pull is when every person has a good grip.

Three types of skeletal muscle fibers

Fibers differ in two ways: how fast they contract (set by how fast their myosin ATPase splits ATP) and how they make ATP (oxidative = aerobic, with oxygen, in mitochondria; glycolytic = anaerobic glycolysis). Most muscles contain all three types, in different proportions.
